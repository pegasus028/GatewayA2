## Module 1: Ordering food
Student's Book p.86 · level A2

**The key idea:** In a café, the waiter offers and asks; you answer politely: *Could I have…, please? / I'd like… / I think I'll have…*

The waiter or waitress says: *Can I help you? Are you ready to order? What can I get you? What would you like? Would you like a drink with that? Small or large? Can I get you anything else? That's 85 baht, please. Here's your change. Enjoy your meal!*

The customer says: *Could I have a chicken burger, please? I'd like a milkshake. I think I'll have a pancake. Have you got any orange juice? How much is that? Can we have the bill, please?*

Grammar inside: *I'd like* = I would like (polite; ✗ *I like a milkshake, please*). After *Could I* use the base verb *have*. For prices, ask *How much…?*, never *How many…?*

Be polite: add *please* and *thank you*. *I want a burger* isn't wrong English, but it can sound rude to a waiter.

Pattern table:
- Waiter / waitress | Customer
- Are you ready to order? | Yes. Could I have the green curry, please?
- Would you like a drink with that? | Yes, I'd like an orange juice, please.
- Small or large? | Large, please.
- Can I get you anything else? | No, thanks. How much is that?
- That's 95 baht, please. | Here you are.
- Here's your change. Enjoy your meal! | Thank you!

Good examples:
- **Could I have** the vegetable rice, please?
- **I'd like** a bottle of water, please.
- **Can we have the bill**, please?

Classic mistakes and their corrections:
- Wrong: "I like a milkshake, please." → Right: "I'd like a milkshake, please."
- Wrong: "Could I having a pancake, please?" → Right: "Could I have a pancake, please?"
- Wrong: "How many is that?" → Right: "How much is that?"

Extra: When you order one drink, *an orange juice* or *a lemonade* is fine — it means one glass or bottle. In other sentences, use the container: *I drink a glass of orange juice every day.*

Memory tip: Learn them in pairs: Ready to order? → Could I have…? / Anything else? → No, thanks.

## Module 2: Invitations
Student's Book p.87 · level A2

**The key idea:** A good invitation says what, when and where — and asks the person to come: *Would you like to come?*

An informal email invitation answers four questions: What's the event? When and where is it? Would you like to come? Do you need to bring anything? (This is just like the A2 Key email task.)

Inviting: *I'm having a party on Saturday. I'm inviting some friends to go to the night market. Would you like to come? Can you come? Please come!* Details: *It starts at 6 pm. We're meeting at the park. Please bring your own drinks. Can you bring some snacks?*

Ending: *Let me know if you can come / can't make it. Hope you can come! See you there! Don't be late!* (*make it* = be able to come)

Grammar traps: *Would you like **to** come?* (✗ *Would you like come?*). *Let me know* (✗ *Let me to know*). *Can you bring* + base verb. Use contractions in a friendly email: *I'm, it's, don't*.

Pattern table:
- Job | Useful expressions
- Invite | I'm inviting some friends to… / Would you like to come? / Can you come? / Please come!
- Time and place | It starts at (7 pm). / We're meeting at (the park).
- What to bring | Please bring (your own drinks). / Can you bring (some food)?
- End | Let me know if you can / can't make it. / Hope you can come! / See you there! / Don't be late!

Good examples:
- Hi Beam, I'm having a pizza party on Friday. **Would you like to come?**
- **Let me know** if you can't make it.

Classic mistakes and their corrections:
- Wrong: "Would you like come to my party?" → Right: "Would you like to come to my party?"
- Wrong: "Let me to know if you can come." → Right: "Let me know if you can come."
- Wrong: "Please bring you own snacks." → Right: "Please bring your own snacks."

Memory tip: Before you send: What? When? Where? What to bring? And one question: Would you like to come?

## Quick challenge for the end of the episode
Question: 
Options: (A) ready for  (B) already  (C) ready  (D) finish
Answer: ready — *Are you ready to order?* means “Do you know what you want?” *Already* sounds similar but means “before now”, and *ready for* needs a noun after it.
