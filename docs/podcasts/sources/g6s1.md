## This stage in one sentence
Can you count it? Learn which food words take a/an and -s — and how to count rice, water and bread.

The stage has 3 modules, in this order: (1) Countable or uncountable?; (2) Counting the uncountable; (3) Tricky nouns.

## Module 1: Countable or uncountable?
Student's Book p.80, p.88 · level A2

**The key idea:** Can you count it — one, two, three? Then it's **countable**: *an egg, two eggs*. If you can't, it's **uncountable**: *some rice*, never *two rices*.

Look at a bowl of grapes. You can count them: one grape, two grapes, three grapes. Now look at a glass of water. Can you say “one water, two waters”? No — water is one thing that you pour. That is the whole difference.

Countable nouns have a singular and a plural: *an apple → apples*, *a carrot → carrots*. In the singular they need *a/an* (or *one, my, the*): ✗ *I ate apple.*

Uncountable nouns have no plural and no *a/an*: *salt, honey, water, rice, butter, sugar, milk, bread*. Use them alone or with *some*: *I'd like some honey.* The verb is singular: *The rice **is** hot.*

Pattern table:
- Countable (egg) | Uncountable (rice)
- one | an egg ✓ | a rice ✗
- more than one | three eggs ✓ | three rices ✗
- some | some eggs ✓ | some rice ✓
- verb | The eggs **are** fresh. | The rice **is** hot.

Good examples:
- There are **some grapes** in the bowl.
- Can you pass the **salt**, please?

Classic mistakes and their corrections:
- Wrong: "I want a rice with my curry." → Right: "I want some rice with my curry."
- Wrong: "I eat two egg every morning." → Right: "I eat two eggs every morning."
- Wrong: "This honey are very sweet." → Right: "This honey is very sweet."

Extra: Some food words can be countable **and** uncountable — *chicken, fish, pizza, cake*. You'll meet them in *Tricky nouns*. In this module we only use words that are always one or the other.

Memory tip: Pour it, spread it or scoop it? Then you probably can't count it: water, butter, rice.

## Module 2: Counting the uncountable
Student's Book p.81, p.88 · level A2

**The key idea:** Want to count something uncountable? Count the **container** or the **piece**: *a bottle of water, two slices of bread*.

You can't say *two waters* or *a bread*. But you can count the things that hold them: one bottle, two bottles. So: *a bottle of water, two bottles of water*.

The pattern is: number + container or piece + **of** + food. The container takes the plural *-s*; the food never changes: *two cartons of milk* (✗ two carton of milks).

Pieces work the same way: *a slice of bread / pizza / toast*, *a piece of fruit*, *a bowl of rice / soup*. Countable food can use containers too: *a box of eggs, a packet of biscuits, a bag of apples*.

Pattern table:
- number | container / piece | of | food
- a | bottle | of | water
- two | cartons | of | milk
- three | slices | of | bread
- a | jar | of | honey
- four | packets | of | biscuits

Good examples:
- Can I have **a glass of water**, please?
- Mum bought **two cartons of milk** at 7-Eleven.
- I had **a bowl of rice** and some green curry.

Classic mistakes and their corrections:
- Wrong: "She ate three slice of breads." → Right: "She ate three slices of bread."
- Wrong: "Can you buy a bottle water?" → Right: "Can you buy a bottle of water?"

Extra: In a café you may hear *Two teas, please* — it means two cups of tea. That's fine when you order a drink, but in tests and in writing, use the container: *two cups of tea*.

Memory tip: The container takes the -s, the food stays the same: two jar**s** of honey.

## Module 3: Tricky nouns
Student's Book p.80, p.89 · level B1 · an EXTRA module (beyond the book, useful for the entrance exam)

**The key idea:** Some food words change meaning: *a chicken* is an animal, *some chicken* is meat. And some words are **never** countable in English: *advice, information, homework, money, news*.

The book asks: can some words go in both groups? Yes! *Chicken, fish, pizza, chocolate* and *cake* can be countable or uncountable. It depends on what you mean.

Countable = one whole thing: *a chicken* (the bird), *a pizza* (the whole round one), *a cake*. Uncountable = some of it, as food: *some chicken*, *a slice of pizza*, *some cake*. *Fish* is special: the plural is usually *fish* — *one fish, three fish*.

Some words are countable in other languages but always uncountable in English: *advice, information, homework, money, news*. No *a/an*, no *-s*. *News* ends in -s but takes a singular verb: *The news **is** good.*

Pattern table:
- Countable (one whole thing) | Uncountable (food)
- chicken | There's a chicken in the garden. | I had some chicken for lunch.
- fish | Tonkla caught two fish. | Fish is good for you.
- pizza | We ordered two pizzas. | Can I have some pizza?
- chocolate | a box of chocolates | a bar of chocolate

Good examples:
- Grandma keeps **three chickens** on her farm.
- Can I ask you for some **advice**?

Classic mistakes and their corrections:
- Wrong: "Can you give me an advice?" → Right: "Can you give me some advice / a piece of advice?"
- Wrong: "I have a lot of homeworks tonight." → Right: "I have a lot of homework tonight."
- Wrong: "The news are on TV at eight." → Right: "The news is on TV at eight."

Extra: *Money* is uncountable, but coins and notes are countable: *a lot of money* ✓, *three coins* ✓, *three moneys* ✗. To count advice or information, say *a piece of advice*, *two pieces of information*.

Memory tip: Five words, never a/an, never a plural: advice, information, homework, money, news. Say them like a chant!

## Quick challenge for the end of the episode
Question: Which group is **honey** in?
Options: (A) Countable  (B) Uncountable  (C) Both  (D) Plural only (like *jeans*)
Answer: Uncountable — *Honey* is uncountable: you can't count it, so there's no *a honey* and no *honeys*. Say *some honey* or *a jar of honey*.
